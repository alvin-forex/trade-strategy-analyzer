1. Bottom line

Do not sign this agreement as drafted. It reads like a standard freelance services contract, but the risk is stacked almost entirely on one side: the contractor takes unlimited liability, can be fired without pay, and gives away their own tools — while the client can withhold invoices at will, amend the contract alone, and drag disputes to a remote arbitration seat with one-way fee shifting. The redline rebalances the document with 12 targeted edits and one new clause (limitation of liability). None of the fixes require the client to give up a commercial deal that is out of market.

3. Analysis of the three largest risks

Uncapped liability (Issue 1). The draft never limits either party's liability, while the warranty, indemnity, and payment clauses all run one way. Combined with a "perfect and error-free" warranty, the contractor's theoretical exposure is the client's entire project-loss claim, with no ceiling. A 12-month fees cap with carve-outs for indemnity, confidentiality, and willful misconduct is the market-standard structure for independent-contractor agreements and does not remove meaningful recovery for the client.

The money clauses (Issues 2 and 6). Net-90 plus discretionary withholding plus instant termination with forfeiture of uninvoiced work means the client can use the contractor's cash flow as free credit and exit without paying for delivered work. The fixes (net-30, good-faith dispute only, pay-for-work-performed) restore ordinary commercial balance.

The IP grab (Issue 3). Assigning the contractor's pre-existing tools — the files they will reuse on every future job — is the clause most likely to be missed on a fast read. The standard resolution is ownership of the deliverables against payment, plus a license (not assignment) of pre-existing materials as embedded in the deliverables.

4. How the review was checked

  • Clause-by-clause pass against common US independent-contractor / freelance agreement practice (payment terms, IP, warranty, indemnity, termination, assignment, dispute resolution, limitation of liability).
  • Cross-issue consistency check: the draft names three jurisdictions at once (California governing law, New York client entity, Honolulu arbitration seat). Jurisdictional conflicts were aligned to one law and one seat.
  • Enforceability flags: a 5-year worldwide non-compete on an individual is void as to California (Bus. & Prof. Code §16600) and overbroad in most other US states; a "perfect and error-free" warranty is effectively uninsurable. Both were rewritten rather than merely flagged.
  • What this review does not do: it is a demonstration on a fictional sample, so it does not opine on worker-classification (employee vs. contractor), tax, or any specific state's statute. Those require the real jurisdiction and the real parties before signing.

5. Reusable checklist (the method)

  • Liability: is there a cap? carve-outs? indirect damages excluded?
  • Money: net how many days? who can withhold, and on what standard? late interest?
  • IP: what happens to my pre-existing tools? license-back present?
  • Warranty: "perfect" promises → professional standards + defined remedy period.
  • Indemnity: mutual? excludes the other side's own fault?
  • Exit: who can walk away, with how much notice, and is work performed paid?
  • Restrictive covenants: duration, geography, scope — and enforceability in the governing state.
  • Disputes: seat, rules, fee-shifting — would I actually be able to fight there?
  • Alignment: one governing law, one venue, consistent with both parties' home bases.
  • Life cycle: auto-renewal notice windows, amendment signature requirement.
